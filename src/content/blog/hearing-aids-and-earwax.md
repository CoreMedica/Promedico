# Hearing Aids and Earwax: Why Build-Up Happens Faster and What to Do About It

If you wear a hearing aid, you've probably noticed that earwax seems to become more of an issue than it used to be. This isn't in your head - hearing aids genuinely change how wax behaves in the ear canal, and left unmanaged, it can quietly undermine the performance of a device you rely on every day.

## Why Hearing Aids Speed Up Wax Build-Up

Earwax is normally self-clearing: chewing, talking and jaw movement gradually work it out of the canal. Hearing aids interfere with that process in a few specific ways:

- **Blocked natural migration** - an in-ear device sits in the same space wax needs to travel through, effectively damming it up
- **Increased wax production** - the ear can respond to the presence of a foreign object by producing more wax, not less
- **Reduced airflow** - a sealed or near-sealed canal is warmer and more humid, which softens wax and encourages it to accumulate rather than clear
- **Physical compaction** - repeatedly inserting and removing a mould or dome can pack wax further into the canal instead of dislodging it

## The Knock-On Effect on Your Hearing Aid

Wax build-up doesn't just affect your ears - it affects the device itself:

- **Whistling or feedback** - often caused by wax altering the seal or blocking the receiver
- **Muffled or reduced sound output** - wax sitting over the microphone or speaker port dampens performance
- **False sense of a "broken" hearing aid** - many people book a device repair or return to their audiologist when the real issue is a blocked canal, not a fault with the aid
- **Increased risk of infection** - trapped wax combined with moisture creates a favourable environment for irritation or infection

## Why Home Removal Methods Are Riskier Here

Hearing aid users are often more inclined to reach for cotton buds, ear candles or over-the-counter wax removal kits - usually because it feels like a quick fix between audiology appointments. But this group carries extra risk:

- Ear canals altered by long-term mould or dome use can be narrower or more sensitive
- Pushing wax further in makes it harder for it to clear naturally next time
- Aggressive removal attempts increase the chance of irritation exactly where a device needs to sit comfortably

## What Professional Removal Looks Like

We use **microsuction** rather than syringing for hearing aid users, for good reason:

| | Microsuction | Ear Syringing (irrigation) |
|---|---|---|
| Moisture | Dry procedure - no water introduced | Water-based, can leave residual moisture |
| Precision | Clinician sees the canal throughout | No direct visualisation |
| Suitability for hearing aid users | Preferred - avoids moisture near devices/skin already adapted to a mould | Higher risk of irritation or delayed drying complications |
| Comfort | Generally more comfortable, no vertigo risk | Can trigger dizziness in some patients |

A microsuction appointment is quick - typically 15–30 minutes for both ears - and leaves the canal completely dry and clear, ready for your hearing aid to go straight back in.

## How Often Should Hearing Aid Users Get Checked?

There's no universal rule, but as a general guide:

1. **Every 3–6 months** for regular hearing aid wearers, even without obvious symptoms
2. **Sooner** if you notice whistling, reduced volume, or a "blocked" sensation
3. **Immediately** if you experience discomfort, discharge, or a sudden drop in hearing

Regular, proactive removal is far kinder to your ears - and your hearing aid - than waiting until a problem forces the issue.
